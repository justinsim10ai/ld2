The retrieval-augmented
web is already here.

Notes from eighteen months on the SEO/AEO frontier — what breaks, what scales, and why the next decade of organic growth belongs to people who can think like a crawler and a model at the same time.

Sometime around the middle of last year, the search team I'd been running stopped thinking about Google and started thinking about retrieval. Not because anyone wrote a memo. Because the numbers told us to.

The traffic from traditional search kept its shape, more or less. But the traffic from AI assistants — citations in Perplexity, references inside ChatGPT answers, recall in Claude responses — went from a curiosity to a measurable slice of pipeline. Roughly 50% of net-new high-intent visits came from somewhere a 2022-era SEO team would not have known to look.

This post is a field report. What follows is opinionated, occasionally wrong, and based on the inside of two crypto platforms and a prediction market — but the pattern generalizes well enough that it feels worth writing down.

The shift nobody scheduled.

Most of the SEO playbook is predicated on a single assumption: a person types a query, scans ten blue links, picks one, and clicks through. Every tactic — keyword targeting, meta titles, schema, link building — is optimization on top of that flow.

Retrieval-augmented generation breaks the assumption at the root. The "user" might be a model. The "click" might be a citation. The "page" might never be visited directly — only ingested, summarized, and quoted back inside another product. If your content isn't structured for the model, you don't lose ranking. You disappear.

i
Definition · AEO

Answer Engine Optimization — the discipline of preparing a site so that LLM-driven answer engines can find, ingest, and accurately cite it. Overlaps with classical SEO at the technical layer, diverges sharply at the content layer.

What "AEO" actually means.

It is tempting — and a little embarrassing — to slap a new acronym on top of whatever you were doing already. So let's be precise. AEO is not "SEO with vibes." Three things distinguish it:

  1. Semantic density over keyword density. Models don't count phrases. They embed them. A page that says one true thing well in 400 words will outperform a page that says the same thing badly in 2,000.
  2. Entity-first architecture. Every page is a node in a graph. The job is to make the relationships between nodes obvious — to crawlers, to models, and to the Knowledge Graph systems that sit between them.
  3. Citation as currency. When an LLM repeats your sentence, it's a citation. When it repeats your competitor's, it isn't. You're not optimizing for clicks; you're optimizing for the probability of being quoted.

None of this is alien to anyone who's done structured-data work, but the emphasis shifts. The schema.org markup you used to bolt on at the end of a project becomes the scaffolding the project hangs from.

— Notes from a Q1 2026 strategy review

A new content stack.

The team I lead now ships about 1,000 new pieces of content per quarter across 24 markets. None of it is "AI slop"; all of it is reviewed by humans before publication. The trick is not LLMs writing pages. The trick is{" "} agentic workflows that handle the 90% of work humans hated anyway — research, normalization, schema generation, internal linking, localization scaffolding.

The pipeline, simplified

⁂ pipeline.yaml
SOURCE   → entity ingestion (knowledge graph)
RESEARCH → multi-agent retrieval + citation gathering
DRAFT    → LLM produces structured outline + body
LINT     → semantic density, schema, internal linking
REVIEW   → human editor + brand pass
PUBLISH  → CMS + RSS + sitemap + LLM-friendly index

The interesting work happens in LINT. That's where you reject drafts that pass a grammar check but fail a model's recall test. We built a reviewer agent whose only job is to ask: "If a user asked an LLM about this topic, would this page be a likely citation?"

1. Treating the Knowledge Graph as a product surface.

We stopped thinking of structured data as decoration and started maintaining it with the same rigor as a production database. Schemas have owners. Entities have versions. Reviews have changelogs. It's slow. It's worth it.

2. Citation seeding through real partnerships.

The fastest way to get a model to recall you is to be co-cited with the entities it already trusts. We invested in multi-channel citation programs with publishers we'd otherwise have ignored. Vector similarity, it turns out, cares about who you stand next to.

3. Building the MCP server.

The crypto MCP we shipped at Crypto.com lets AI assistants query live wallet, price, and on-chain data through a single typed interface. It is arguably more important to our long-term discoverability than any content we've written, because it makes the platform directly addressable by agents.

Three things that didn't.

  • Mass programmatic SEO. The 2018 playbook of generating ten thousand thin pages for the long tail does not survive contact with answer engines. They flatten the long tail by definition. We killed about 40% of our programmatic content in Q1 and traffic went up.
  • Hand-tuned meta titles. A small team can fuss over a few hundred meta titles a quarter. Across 24 markets and 1,000 pieces, you cannot. Letting the agent set them, with human review on the high-stakes 5%, was strictly better.
  • Treating AI search as "another channel". It isn't a channel. It's the substrate the next channel will be built on. Reporting on it as if it were Bing — "this is the AI column" — encourages the wrong tradeoffs.

What I'd do tomorrow.

If I were starting fresh on a content team in 2026, this is the order I'd build things in:

  1. Audit your entity coverage before you write anything. If your top ten brand entities aren't cleanly modeled in your CMS, fix that first. Everything else compounds off it.
  2. Stand up an MCP server for whatever your platform is. Even a small one. The act of designing it forces the rest of your data architecture to clean up.
  3. Wire your editorial review to a model judge. Not as an oracle — as a second opinion. Disagree with it on purpose. Track when you were right and when it was.
  4. Measure citation, not just traffic. Tools like Profound make this tractable. If you're not measuring it, you can't tell whether you're winning.
  5. Hire one engineer per content lead. The bottleneck is no longer writing. It's the connective tissue between your CMS, your schema, your reviewer agents, and your reporting.
⁂
A note on tooling

I use Claude Code, Profound, and a handful of internal tools daily. None of this is sponsored; I just got tired of pretending you can do this work without writing code anymore.

The retrieval-augmented web is not a future state. It's the medium we're already operating in — most teams just haven't relabeled their dashboards yet. The teams that do will find that the work feels surprisingly familiar: write one true thing, structure it well, get it cited, repeat.

The only difference is the reader isn't always human anymore.
